How this estimate works

What it estimates

The general property tax on this home for one full year, divided by 12. That is the property tax part of a monthly escrow payment. It is an estimate of the buyer's tax under the occupancy you select, not the seller's current bill. The result panel names every term in the calculation and says where each one came from.

The steps

  1. Find the parcel. The address is turned into a street location, then matched against the statewide parcel layer maintained by the Utah Geospatial Resource Center (UGRC), which covers all 29 counties. You see the matched parcel number, county and recorded address so you can confirm it is the right property.
  2. Pick a point inside the parcel. The tool uses a point inside the parcel boundary, not the street location, so a house near a district line gets the right districts.
  3. Find every taxing entity covering that point. Utah property tax is the sum of rates levied by overlapping bodies: the county, the school district, the city or town, and special districts such as water, sewer, fire, library and vector control. Their boundaries and rates come from the Utah State Tax Commission's tax entity data.
  4. Add the rates. The sum is the combined rate for that tax area. Utah writes rates as decimals: 0.009240 means $9.24 per $1,000 of taxable value, or 0.924%.
  5. Apply the rate to taxable value. Taxable value is the market value, reduced by the residential exemption when the home will be a primary residence. The market value used is the county assessor's recorded value where the county publishes one, and your purchase price where it does not; when both are available the panel shows the two side by side.

The formula

Market value = county assessor's value, or your price Taxable value = market value × 55% (primary residence) = market value × 100% (anything else) Annual tax = taxable value × combined rate Monthly tax = annual tax ÷ 12

The primary residential exemption

Utah taxes a primary residence on 55% of its market value (a 45% exemption, Utah Code 59-2-1035). Second homes, short-term rentals and most investment property are taxed on 100%, so their tax is close to double. The exemption covers the home and up to one acre of land per unit. On a larger lot the land beyond one acre is taxed at full value, so the estimate runs slightly low; the tool flags lots over one acre.

Which value the estimate uses

A bill is built on the assessor's market value, not on the sale price. The county's value is re-set every January 1 and does not move automatically when a home sells. When the county record carries a value, the estimate uses it and shows your price next to it as a comparison. Where the county publishes no value, the price stands in, and the panel says so. For reference, the Tax Commission's assessment-to-sales ratio study (2023) found the county's value sat at 95.5% of sale price in Salt Lake, 95.4% in Utah, 95.8% in Davis, 97.5% in Weber, 94.9% in Washington and near 100% in Cache and Tooele, with the state requiring the typical ratio to fall between 90% and 110%. So a typed price is usually a few percent high, not wildly wrong.

If you are comparing it with a listing or someone's bill

Two things shift between a notice and a purchase. Occupancy: the number on a listing or a seller's notice reflects the seller's occupancy. A house the seller rented is taxed on 100% of its value; the same house lived in by its owner is taxed on 55%. A change of occupancy at purchase moves the tax by close to half, in either direction, which is a bigger effect than anything else on this page. Timing: value and rate are both fixed as of January 1, a sale does not re-set the value on its own, and new improvements are normally added to the roll at the following January 1, so the first year after a purchase or a new build can land away from both figures shown here.

Which rates, and for which year

"Rate year" is the tax year the rates come from. Utah entities certify new rates in the fall and the Tax Commission publishes them over the winter, so for much of a given year the newest published rates are for the tax year before the one now in progress. Rates usually drift up a little each year; the county assessor can tell you what is proposed going forward.

How the rates were checked

The summed rates were compared with Report 600, the Utah State Tax Commission's list of certified and adopted rates by tax area, which is what assessors and treasurers work from. Sampling parcels across Salt Lake, Utah and Davis counties, the summed entity rate equalled the Commission's Final Adopted Tax Rate for the tax area on 75 of 75 sampled points for tax year 2024 and 73 of 75 for 2025. The two misses were parcels sitting on area boundaries that the state's entity maps do not draw exactly.

What is not included

  • Special assessments and improvement district charges billed with the tax notice, such as streets, sewers or irrigation paid off against property.
  • City fees some counties collect on the tax notice, such as stormwater or street lighting.
  • Personal exemptions and relief: disabled veteran, blind, active duty military, circuit breaker and similar.
  • Delinquent tax, penalties, or rollback tax on land leaving greenbelt status.
  • Land over one acre: the residential exemption stops at one acre, so a large lot is taxed on more of its value than a simple 55% and the estimate runs low. Lots over one acre are flagged.
  • Tax increment financing effects. A community reinvestment or development area redirects part of the rate to another taxing entity; it does not change what the owner pays.

Checks the tool runs while it works

  • It warns if the parcel falls inside more than one city or more than one school district, which means it sits on a boundary the state data does not draw exactly.
  • It warns when no school district or county levy is found, which means a gap in the state data rather than a real zero.
  • It warns when the address could only be matched approximately, and lists the next best parcels so you can switch.
  • Reinvestment and development areas are listed with a zero rate. They change where the money goes, not how much the owner pays.

Data sources

  • Parcels: UGRC statewide parcel layer, UtahStatewideParcels, with county layers used for the recorded market value and acreage where the county publishes them.
  • Rates and boundaries: Utah State Tax Commission tax entity polygons, published by UGRC. Boundaries are approximate, not legal descriptions.
  • Street locations: the ESRI World Geocoding Service, used only to turn your address into a point.
  • Rates cross-checked against Utah State Tax Commission Report 600.
  • To confirm a specific bill, use the county treasurer's records or the title report.
